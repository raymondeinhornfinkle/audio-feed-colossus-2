Lovely Day for the Black Stuff

Guinness used to be an old man's pint, poured in old men's pubs. Now the world is drinking less, yet the Irish stout is selling faster than ever.

By Alex Bilmes.

First, select a pint glass. Now hold it up to the light. Clean won’t do; it must be spotless. Any fading lipstick stain, lingering food residue, the faintest greasy smudge or evidence of unrinsed detergent can contaminate the product, compromising the taste. To avoid this, your glass should have been washed and rinsed, separately from other beer or wine glasses, or cutlery or crockery, and then thoroughly drip-dried so not a hint of moisture remains. It should be a branded glass, naturally, with the name of the product and its logo printed on the side. All good? Now to the pour.

Take a firm stance, shoulders back, and hold your glass at 45 degrees to the tap. The nozzle should be close to the rim but not touching. Now pull the tap towards you, all the way down, and commence the pour. As the liquid fills the glass, you’ll hear the whistle of nitrogen releasing and you’ll see churning brown clouds rolling down and then rising again as bubbles, three million of them in every pour. This is called the “surge.” Next, slowly tilt the glass so that the creamy head begins to level out.

Here you must use your judgement to gauge the correct moment to straighten the glass completely, just as the head reaches the top of the harp logo. Now close the tap. Place the glass on the bar and leave it to settle for 119.5 seconds. Watch as the separation begins between the dark beer at the bottom and the light head at the top, a shift that will take the liquid from murky sepia to noirish high-contrast black and white.

Now return the glass to the tap, upright, and hold it steady under the nozzle. Push the tap gently away from you so that the liquid plunges straight through the head, filling the glass to the brim. You’re hoping for a slight dome at the head, like a well-risen cake. Turn the glass so that the logo is facing away from you, towards the customer. Place it on a beer mat, on the bar. And there it is, a pint of Guinness, perfectly poured and presented.

If you’re feeling generous and hospitable, you could add an Irish “Cheers!” “Sláinte!” (Pronounced “slawn-cha.”)

I’m telling you this with authority not because I work in a pub but because I am recently returned from a visit to the Connoisseur Bar, on the fourth floor of the Guinness Storehouse in Dublin, where I was taught the technique.

The Storehouse, which opened to the public in 2000 on the site of the St James’s Gate Brewery, where Guinness has brewed beer since 1759, is Ireland’s number one paid tourist attraction. Approached on foot, through the streets of the area known as the Liberties, you smell St James’s Gate before you see it: a rich aroma that turns out to be predominately the scent of roasted barley, one of only four ingredients that go into making Guinness. (The others are water, yeast, and hops.) The Storehouse is arranged over seven floors in a building where, from 1902 to 1988, the process of fermentation took place. It houses a permanent exhibition on the history, science, and marketing of the beer. From the seventh floor looking south, you can see Brewhouse 4, where three million pints of Guinness are produced every day.

In the Connoisseur Bar, I hitched my wagon to the train of a group of middle-aged Texans in weather-appropriate technical gear; outside, a gentle rain was falling, misting the view of the Wicklow Mountains in the distance. My new friends had detoured from their Scottish golfing vacation for a weekend in the Irish capital. Like me, they’d paid a premium for the privilege of pulling their own pints. Sportingly they allowed me to join their pouring tutorial, a kindness I repaid by becoming briefly their personal videographer.

We took our instructions from a friendly Nepalese woman in a Guinness polo shirt. When we’d all successfully filled our glasses, eyes narrowed in concentration, we drank deeply, even though it was only 10.30am on a Friday morning and I for one had a long day’s supping ahead of me.

The pouring, it turns out, is not difficult, even for clumsy oafs such as myself. It can’t be, otherwise I would not now be in possession of a document, signed and dated, that reads “This is to certify that Alex Bilmes has poured a beautiful pint of Guinness,” with my name printed in a pleasing cursive script. Before all this goes to my head, the technique I was taught, the “double pour,” with the glass partially filled and then topped up, is not without its critics.

There are those, a Dublin landlord told me, who favor a single pour. Even a triple pour. Others, including a publican in my own hometown, London, suggested that none of it makes a bit of difference to the taste of the thing. “It’s just marketing,” he said. (He went on to insist, with some passion, that nevertheless, in his pub only a double pour is acceptable, because “it’s tradition.”)

The Dublin landlord told me that he felt that the last part of the double pour, when you push the tap away from you, was cheating. It indicated a lack of timing and dexterity. Both landlords felt the glass I had used at the Guinness Storehouse, known as the Gravity glass—the official branded glassware since 2011—was an offence to serious Guinness drinking.

The true Guinness lover sneers at the Gravity glass, I was told, with its embossed harp and trophy-like sculpted lines. One landlord suggested to me, with a curled lip, that it looks like a cheap tourist’s model of the football World Cup. Online chatter confirms this prejudice. Aficionados prefer its predecessor, the wider, more curvaceous Tulip glass—the Platonic ideal of a pint glass—which is felt to be more photogenic, a crucial requirement for a beer glass in the age of Instagram. “Don’t get me started on glasses,” said the Dubliner.

If there was a common thread that ran through all my conversations for this story, it was that nothing, in Guinness world, is black and white. That goes, too, for the beer itself—which is, if you look closely, a deep ruby red.

What is undisputed: for the past five years, at least since the middle of the Covid pandemic, Guinness has been the hot story in beer. In Ireland and the UK, where the resurgence has been most marked, but also in the U.S. and around the world, Guinness has seen an explosion in popularity, achieving double-digit growth for its owner, the multinational drinks company Diageo, consistently bucking trends and confounding statisticians.

A decade ago, few trendspotters would have selected Guinness for hip Millennial and Gen Z brand appeal. Pre-Covid, the notion that a dark Irish stout, centuries old, might become a social media sensation, then a global craze, would have seemed preposterous. Now that we’ve absorbed the idea, Guinness seems ready-made for the era of the clip, the meme, and the reel.

In June, at a famous Dublin pub known as The Gravediggers, I drank Guinness with Ciaran Kavanagh, whose family has been serving Guinness to working class locals since the nineteenth century. They still do that, but now they also serve it to Japanese TV news crews and Italian TikTok influencers and even—burp—English magazine journalists. I asked Kavanagh if he was surprised by this unlikely turn of events.

“Surprised?” he said. “I’m amazed by it.”

James Joyce called it the wine of Ireland. It’s not clear if the author of some of the canon’s most challenging fiction meant this approvingly, or if he was being mischievous. (Joyce lived almost all his life outside Ireland, 20 years of it in Paris, France, where the wine of the nation is wine.)

In Joyce’s day, Guinness was Irish-owned. Since 1997, when it merged with the spirit-maker Grand Metropolitan to form Diageo—owner of a heady cocktail of drinks brands including Johnnie Walker, Smirnoff, and Tanqueray—Guinness has been headquartered in London.

Its founding father is Arthur Guinness, born in 1725 in County Kildare, west of Dublin. Arthur’s father, Richard Guinness, worked for the Archbishop of Cashel. Among his responsibilities was the supervision of the brewing of beer for workers on the estate.

Following in his father’s footsteps, Arthur Guinness used the £100 he was bequeathed by the archbishop, in 1752, to establish a small brewery in nearby Leixlip. Seven years later, aged 34, he signed a 9,000-year lease on a then-disused brewery site at St James’s Gate, Dublin, for an annual rent of £45, approximately £11,000 ($14,500) in today’s money. That document, the original lease, is displayed beneath reinforced glass in the floor of the Guinness Storehouse. Like a penitent, I had to sink to my knees to get a good look at the curly signature, trademarked in 1876 along with the harp logo and the all-caps GUINNESS “wordmark,” still used on labels today.

Arthur Guinness took possession of St James’s Gate on the last day of 1759. He began by brewing ale and quickly became successful. In the 1770s he tried producing a new type of English beer, porter. Developed in London, porter—named for the sturdy market workers who favored it—was dark in color, owing to the brown malt used, and hoppy to the taste. It was a step forward, because it was sent from brewery to pub in wooden casks, ready to be bottled and consumed. Previously ale and brown beer had been delivered to publicans to age and mix themselves. Now the brewers had more influence over their products’ flavor. Porter was also referred to as plain—and still is in Ireland. “A pint of plain is your only man,” according to Flann O’Brien’s poem, “The Workman’s Friend.” That’s how it was regarded, and perhaps still is, in Ireland at least. It developed into stout, the chief difference between the two being that in stout, the barley is roasted.

The first known export of Guinness’s Dublin Porter left Dublin Port on a ship bound for England on May 19, 1796, with just six barrels on board. Three years later, so popular had it become that Arthur Guinness brewed his last ale and decided to concentrate solely on the black stuff.

In 1801, he brewed his first West India Porter, specifically designed to withstand long sea voyages, with a higher hop and alcohol content. It first arrived in Barbados in 1822, beginning an ongoing relationship between Irish stout and the beer drinkers of the Caribbean. Today, a distinctive local recipe remains popular across the West Indies: Guinness Punch, in which a can of beer is mixed with condensed milk, raw egg, nutmeg, and vanilla. Some specify adding a teaspoon of dark rum, others call for cinnamon. All claim the concoction promotes vitality and libido; a sort of proto energy drink.

Soon Guinness was exporting to the U.S., mainland Europe, and West Africa using a fleet of ships called the Guinness Ladies. By the 1880s St James’s Gate had become the largest brewery in the world and expanded to cover 65 acres.

Arthur’s grandson, the formidable Benjamin Guinness, transformed Guinness into a global concern. It’s Benjamin’s death that provides the motor for the plot of the recent Netflix series, House of Guinness; if you like your period dramas with a techno soundtrack, bone-crunching violence, and a cavalier approach to the facts, then Peaky Blinders creator Steven Knight’s show is the Guinness story for you.

What House of Guinness does accurately reflect is the position the aristocratic Anglo-Irish dynasty of brewers, bankers and, occasionally, hellraisers has held in British high society since the 19th century. It is a family tree with many branches. At the top today is the Earl of Iveagh, 57-year-old Edward Guinness, known as Ned. He lives at Elveden Hall, an 18th-century stately home situated in 17,000 acres of Suffolk countryside. Other well-known family members (living) include the socialite Daphne Guinness, a sort of avant garde Cruella de Vil; and Jasmine Guinness, a model and fashion designer.

In 1886, under Benjamin’s son Edward Cecil Guinness, the first Earl of Iveagh, the company went public, valued at £6mn. By 1914 and the outbreak of war in Europe, Guinness was producing more than double the amount of beer of its nearest competitor, Bass. (Between 1936 and 2005, British Guinness was brewed at the Park Royal brewery in west London, at its peak the largest and most productive brewery in the world. Now it crosses the Irish Sea from Dublin.)

The first Guinness brewery outside Ireland and Great Britain opened in Ikeja, Lagos, Nigeria, in 1962. Today Nigeria is the fourth largest market for Guinness behind the UK, Ireland, and the U.S. The beer in Nigeria is not the same, and is much stronger: Guinness Foreign Extra Stout (around 7.5% ABV compared to 4.2% ABV for Guinness Draught) uses maize instead of barley.

But the most significant innovation in Guinness’s history occurred in 1959, as the growth in the popularity of draught versus bottled beer imperiled Guinness sales. Until then it was much more complicated to pull a pint of stout than a bitter or lager. Guinness sold as draught beer was poured using a system called “High and Low.” There were two casks, one typically on the bar or at head height above it and one down below, by the barman’s knees. The High cask contained freshly brewed, carbonated beer. Opening the tap produced a rush of thick foam, which would fill up to three quarters of the glass. That would be set aside for at least a minute to settle. The Low cask contained older stout, which had lost its fizz. This was added for the flavor, and mixed. Each time a pint was served, the barman was essentially blending the beer in real time. It was time consuming and made for wildly inconsistent flavors and consistency. Small wonder Guinness was most popular in bottles.

A 1933 Guinness poster by John Gilroy.

Michael Ash, a mathematician turned brewer, had the idea of introducing nitrogen, in addition to carbon dioxide—in a ratio of approximately 75 to 25%—into the dispensing system. The nitrogen creates smaller, denser bubbles that float downwards before rising to create the creamy head. The beer could now be poured from a single barrel, under higher pressure, because nitrogen doesn’t dissolve as easily as CO2. In Ireland especially, publicans developed different ideas about the correct ratio of nitrogen to CO2 (should it be 80 to 20? Or 78 to 22?), the exact temperature the beer should be stored at (six degrees Celsius? Seven degrees? Eight degrees?), the correct number of days to wait before “tapping” a keg (three? Five? Seven?), and how often the lines should be cleaned (every 3 or 4 weeks?).

One area of near-consensus: what a drinker is looking for in a pint of Guinness. A head height of 12mm to 18mm, slightly domed. A healthy “shtick,” the residue of the head that attaches to the side of the glass as you drink and ideally remains there even when you’ve finished. A soft, creamy, velvety mouthfeel. The taste should be mildly bitter (that’s the hops), and just a little sweet (that’s the barley) with a slight aroma of coffee and dark chocolate.

Done right it is, as millions of cream-mustached drinkers can testify, quite exceptionally moreish.

As a boy growing up in Cork in the 2010s, the social media influencer Ian Ryan felt that “Guinness represented the old Ireland.”

“I just never considered it something that a young person would drink,” he writes in A Beautiful Pint, his book about searching for the perfect pour.

The teenage Ryan’s attitude was typical of his generation. In 2014, Mintel, the market research agency, published a report which opened with a stark observation: “Simply put, Guinness is no longer cool.”

It was an old man’s drink, served in old men’s pubs, to old men. Its reputation, especially among younger drinkers, and women of all ages, even in its biggest markets of Ireland and the UK, was that Guinness was hearty, heavy—a “meal in a glass.” (In fact, it’s relatively low in calories compared to other beers: a pint of a standard lager, Budweiser for instance, contains 232 calories, compared to Guinness’s 210. IPAs, with ABVs of above 5%, can sometimes contain more than 300 calories.)

At 53, I don’t consider myself an old man, but I’m of a generation ahead of Ryan’s. My own first memory of Guinness, from 1978, is of my dad loading a crate of bottles into the back of the family car to take to my mother, who was in hospital recovering from the birth of my youngest brother. My mum was not a beer drinker, I never once saw her lift a pint of Guinness or anything else, but stout was felt at that time to be a valuable source of iron, and it was customary for British and Irish doctors to prescribe it to new mothers. Blood donors were also encouraged, back then, to sink a pint of Guinness. It was medicinal.

As a beer-drinking Brit, coming of age in the late 1980s and early 1990s, I can’t recall having had strong feelings about Guinness, either way. I had tried it and liked it well enough. But it wasn’t on my radar as I bellied up to the bar to get another round in. My friends and I drank European lager, at home and in the pub. We were not alone. Ciaran Kavanagh of The Gravediggers is two years my senior. He remembers: “I wouldn’t touch a pint of Guinness in my late teens, early twenties. Wouldn’t even think of it.” And he grew up in rooms above a pub famous for its Guinness.

Hipsters, the influencers of the 2000s, didn’t drink Guinness, either. Dressed in “ironic” trucker hats and branded workwear, they drank American lagers—Pabst Blue Ribbon—and, later, IPAs and craft lagers. They didn’t go to Irish pubs. They went to independent brewhouses, microbreweries. They wanted limited edition rather than mass market, exclusive rather than democratic.

Other beers, you can buy the bottle or the can and you pour it into the glass and it tastes pretty similar to what you have in a pub. I think Guinness, when it’s done correctly, in draught, is completely unique and special and only works in a pub environment, or a bar environment, with the ritual of the pour, the theatre of it, and the correct temperature and the bubbles and the cream. That’s important because in the pandemic nobody could get that. And I think people craved it. And social media supercharged that craving.

– Oisín Rogers

Diageo’s attempts to make Guinness sexy, to appeal to younger drinkers and women, were hit and miss. Guinness American Blonde Lager didn’t go down as well as hoped. Hop House 13 Lager was more successful. All such innovations were unwelcome to some of the brand’s more traditional consumers. The British food writer Tim Hayward summed up the feeling of the veteran Guinness drinker in a 2009 article for The Guardian, in response to a trial of a “mid-strength” version of the beer.

“They’ve spent years now building a brand that’s in complete opposition to cheap lagers, session drinking and crowds of young men boozing in bars. They’ve worked very hard to help Guinness drinkers picture themselves as twinkly eyed, Byronic bar-room intellectuals, sitting quietly with a pint and dreaming of poetry and impossibly lovely redheads running barefoot across the peat. You have a pint or two of Guinness with a slim volume of Yeats, not eight mates and a 19-pint bender which ends in tattoos, A&E and herpes from a hen party.” Clearly, Hayward had never visited Dublin’s Temple Bar on a summer Saturday.

When the pandemic hit, in 2020, it portended disaster for a beer that relied so heavily on “on-trade sales”—pints pulled in bars and pubs, rather than cans bought at supermarkets for home consumption. On-trade made up more than 50% of European sales, against an average of 35% for other beers, and the pandemic restrictions caused an immediate plunge in sales.

But in 2022, with pubs and bars reopening around the world, Guinness reported double-digit growth in the UK, Ireland and across North America. The following year, sales were up 10% globally, and 19% in the UK, and Guinness became for the first time the best-selling beer in British pubs, with one in every nine pints sold. (Today it’s one in every six.) By Christmas 2024 Diageo announced a nationwide shortage across the UK. So restricted was supply that pubs in London put up handmade signs indicating that they would sell Guinness to regulars only.

Suddenly, Guinness converts from Ed Sheeran to every quarter-zip-wearing fintech bro you’ve ever met were on TikTok trying to “split the G”: attempting to drink exactly the right amount of stout so that when you set your pint down the line between beer and head precisely bisects the G in Guinness, on the side of the glass. At 260 years young, Guinness had gone viral.

No one knows precisely when splitting the G was invented. The game was played in Irish pubs long before the pandemic and the phrase had entered the Urban Dictionary by 2018. But in 2022, the hashtag #SplitTheG went viral, helping to power the surge across the Atlantic and around the world. In U.S. cities with large Irish American communities especially, Guinness sales exploded. By 2024, Mike O’Sullivan, owner of the Brooklyn pub Hartley’s, told the Daily Mail, Guinness had gone “completely bananas.” It is now the most popular draught beer in New York City and Boston and fastest-growing imported beer in the country, according to Nielsen.

Remarkably, this resurgence has played out against the backdrop of plummeting alcohol sales worldwide and near-apocalyptic conditions in the hospitality trade. In the UK, where Guinness has done best, data shows alcohol consumption at a record low, according to figures from research company IWSR, published last December in the Financial Times. Those who do drink report drinking less, and less frequently. In Britain two pubs close for good every day, according to the British Beer and Pub Association.

In the U.S. the figures are, if anything, even more striking. In August 2025 a Gallup poll showed the number of American adults who say they drink alcohol at all fell to 54%. (The figure was 62 in 2023 and 58 in 2024.) The fall is more marked among young adults, only one in two of whom reports consuming any alcohol, and 18–34s in particular are much more likely to regard alcohol as a health risk. This is no surprise: increasingly, the message from doctors is shifting from promoting moderate alcohol intake—red wine for heart health, for example—to the firm opinion that no amount of alcohol consumption is safe.

And yet, Guinness thrives. In the summer of 2025 the American pop star Olivia Rodrigo, herself a pint-sized sensation, performed in Dublin wearing a T-shirt that read “Guinness is Good 4 U”—a reference to “Guinness is Good for You,” the brand’s 1929 slogan, and one of her hit songs. Guinness isn’t good for you (or even 4 U). But Rodrigo is one of a trio of glamorous global trendsetters to have said to hell with that and been photographed enjoying a pint in a pub, demonstrating their alleged love of “the craic.” The others are the queen of all media, Kim Kardashian, and the Princess of Wales—neither woman known to be a hardened beer drinker. (Kardashian is many things, but she’s hardly Norm from Cheers—though that is an SNL sketch I’d be pleased to see.)

So popular has Guinness become that it has minted its own stars. Daragh Curran, aka The Guinness Guru, is a personable young Irish YouTuber whose videos regularly receive over 120k views. “It’s pretty much just me going around, drinking pints, talking shite,” is his candid assessment. His reviews operate on a similarly opaque scoring system to that used by social media titans such as Dave Portnoy, of One Bite Pizza. The Guinness at famous Dublin bar Bowe’s, for example, scores 9.2 and the accompanying review is impressive in its concision: “Holy fucking shit.” For the avoidance of doubt, this is high praise.

Jason Hackett is better known to his 220k Instagram followers and 25k YouTube subscribers as Prime Mutton. A portly, mustachioed Mancunian in a pork pie hat, with an apparently inexhaustible supply of colorful polo shirts, which he wears under suspenders, Hackett’s slightly gormless affect is belied by his status as a former world champion bridge player. He travels the world sinking endless pints for those of us too time-poor, or health-conscious, or simply not brave or stupid enough to do it for ourselves. A pint Hackett judges to be very good is a “creamer,” a truly great pint is an “absolute creamer.” Asked by a British website to describe his own appeal, Hackett settled on “wholesome and jolly, not too polished.” Which seems about right.

Perhaps the most Guinnfluential (Lord forgive me) of all is Ian Ryan, of @shitlondonguinness. What started as a Twitter account “documenting all of the shite pints of Guinness in London” now has 240k followers on Instagram. Ryan also runs a more obliging spin-off—@beautifulpints—and his book about Guinness is published by Bloomsbury, home to Harry Potter.

Oisín Rogers, landlord of The Devonshire, the London pub that sells more Guinness than any other in the world (an incredible 20,000 pints each week, year-round, sometimes up to 25,000, sometimes 5,000 pints in a single day) credits Ryan, in particular, with helping to drive the UK resurgence.

“He was highlighting the places where people weren’t doing it well,” Rogers told me. “That was accidentally the catalyst for the beer itself improving beyond measure across London and then it spread from there. I think landlords went, ‘What the fuck? Does anyone know how to do Guinness properly? Can someone come and fix this and train me and my staff?’ There was a positive contagion of improvement in what you were getting. And I suggest that before the pandemic up to 80% of pints of Guinness in [London] were below par—and that’s me being kind.”

While the Guinnfluencers surely played their parts, the story of how we got from there (“Guinness is no longer cool”) to here (hottest brand in beer) is more complicated. Does the bulk of the credit go to the efforts of the Diageo marketing department? Is it all down to the enforced abstinence induced by the pandemic? Is it videos of Jason Momoa splitting the G? Or the collaboration with Northern Irish fashion designer JW Anderson? Or is it, as the authors of the unofficial The Little Guide to Guinness suggest, the result of younger drinkers’ “yearning for authenticity in an increasingly insincere world?” Is it all of the above? Or is it just: right beer, right time?

The night before my trip to Dublin, I took Charlie Baker for a few pints of rather good Guinness at my local gastropub, the Hawthorn, in Shepherd’s Bush, west London. Baker is the editor of a much talked-about magazine, The Fence, which waspishly surveys the social trends of London’s chattering classes. Last year, he published a funny book, The Pub, about “Britain’s best-loved establishment.”

“First, I think it’s important to say it’s a fantastic product,” Baker told me, when I asked him to explain Guinness’s appeal. An Englishman who went to university at Trinity, Dublin, Baker says that when he arrived in the Irish capital, in 2008, not one of his cohort of Brits—“10 guys, eight girls”—was a Guinness drinker. By the time they’d completed their degrees all but one had abandoned lagers and gin and tonics in favor of Guinness. “You know what you’re getting with Guinness,” he said. “It’s so reliable. It’s like Coca-Cola: it’s just the best option. It’s a fantastic drink all year round. It’s low in alcohol. If the lines are clean and the pipes are well maintained and the temperature is right, you’re going to get a good pint. And then it also has this great heritage.”

“I think Guinness was always going to have a massive renaissance,” says Oisín Rogers, on a recent Friday morning at his pub in Soho, where the 11am rush to the bar is already underway. “There’s a number of really good reasons for that,” Rogers tells me. “They’re related to the product. One is that it’s something you really can’t do at home. Other beers, you can buy the bottle or the can and you pour it into the glass and it tastes pretty similar to what you have in a pub. I think Guinness, when it’s done correctly, in draught, is completely unique and special and only works in a pub environment, or a bar environment, with the ritual of the pour, the theatre of it, and the correct temperature and the bubbles and the cream. That’s important because in the pandemic nobody could get that. And I think people craved it. And social media supercharged that craving.”

A garrulous, curly-haired Irishman in his mid-fifties, Rogers—Osh, to those who know him—has earned the right to pronounce on the brand’s appeal. A London publican since he was 20 years old, before he opened The Devonshire he sold Guinness at the esteemed The Guinea Grill in Mayfair, and before that The Ship in Wandsworth, both famous for their hospitality, and the quality of their beer.

The Devonshire, a “proper pub,” as Rogers calls it, over four floors on a corner near Piccadilly Circus, opened at the end of 2023 to near unanimous critical acclaim and unprecedented success. At the time, I worked in an office building maybe three minutes’ walk from the Dev, as everyone calls it. Overnight, the pub became a London landmark, as well as a phenomenon, in real life, online, and in the media, social and legacy; I wrote a story about it for British Esquire. You can go to The Devonshire for an elaborate steak dinner—the food is terrific—or you can stop in for a pint, and potentially never leave. The ground floor bar operates at capacity almost all day every day. No points for guessing what they’re drinking.

“The flavor profile of Guinness, what you actually taste, is quite bland,” says Rogers. “It’s not that exciting. And that’s a really good thing because it doesn’t complicate your palate when you’re drinking it. It doesn’t become anything that’s overbearing, like an IPA does. It’s smooth, it goes down well. It’s not particularly high in alcohol, either. And you finish the pint, and you just want another one.” Or more than one.

Finally, Rogers believes that in a polarized world, arguing the toss over whether Guinness is better in Dublin than it is in The Devonshire, or which glass ought to be used, or what temperature is ideal, or whether splitting the G is a stupid idea or good clean fun, is a welcome diversion from weightier and more fraught conversations about politics and society. “For me,” he says, “that feeds into the idea that Guinness is a very benevolent brand. It’s very tangible and authentic.”

Baker, of The Fence and The Pub, thinks there is another force at play. “As someone who has drunk Guinness for 17 years and who doesn’t go to pubs that don’t have Guinness, I’m very attuned to the weird fetishization of Irishness, and Irish people,” he says.

In contemporary British pop culture, Irish creatives are overrepresented—the novelist Sally Rooney, the Dublin rock band Fontaines DC, and an entire red (or green) carpet of Hollywood film stars, led by Cillian Murphy, Colin Farrell, Paul Mescal, Barry Keoghan, and Saoirse Ronan—while Baker likens the experience of Irish friends of his in London now to that of Englishmen in New York in the 1990s. In short, Irishness is cool, and Guinness is cool by association.

Partly, Baker suggests, this is a consequence of the fact that, as he puts it, “Englishness, not to me but to others, is seen as embarrassing and weird.” To a certain demographic, he argues—educated, metropolitan, progressive—expressing pride in one’s English heritage has come to be seen as reactionary, right wing, even bigoted. At least some of this sentiment is linked to one’s feelings about the populist politician Nigel Farage, formerly the chief cheerleader for Brexit.

“I think it’s ridiculous, but Englishness is seen as flag-adjacent, Farage-coded,” says Baker. “And Irishness is seen as romantic and interesting, and the sort of thing that cool young people want to be associated with. Of course, people choose to overlook the fact that Guinness was owned by a famous Anglo-Irish dynasty for years, and now it’s [owned by] a multinational corporation, and yet somehow it’s still seen as quite an earthy, rootsy brand.”

Others tell me they feel Irishness has always been cool, and that the values associated with it—warmth, authenticity—have long been appealing. But I think Baker’s on to something. Irish pubs were the antithesis of cool when I was growing up in London. Now you have to fight your way to the bar.

Gráinne Wafer, Diageo’s category director for beer, and as such Guinness’s grand vizier, has her own ideas about the brand’s recent success. Wafer is the daughter of a Dublin publican, whose establishment was just across the road from St James’s Gate, so she has Guinness in her blood. On a Teams call a few days after my pint with Osh Rogers, I asked for her analysis of Guinness’s success.

She said that the Mintel report of 2014 had been a “sobering read” and that Diageo recognized then that the strategy for Guinness needed a reset.

“Our drinker profile had become older, we weren’t recruiting women to the brand, we were primarily being drunk in an on-trade, traditional environment.” That’s marketing-speak for “old man’s pub.” “We were primarily being drunk in winter.”

“And then against that context,” Wafer continued, “you have a consumer base that is changing the way it’s socializing, still going to the pub but thinking about non-traditional venues, what we call third spaces, like concerts, festivals. We needed to appeal more to the next generation of drinkers, and also to women.”

The first order of business, she said, was to make sure the best version of the product was being served. “Guinness leaves the brewery after 284 quality checks but obviously the last mile of the delivery is really important: glassware, crafted presentations, the gas mix, the chilling, line cleaning. We needed to up the standards. So we’ve been working with [pubs, bars] since that time on improving the quality of Guinness.”

She also shifted marketing and advertising spend from TV commercials, for which Guinness was once celebrated, to social media. “And then just as we’re getting going on this big brand reset, Covid hit. When the bars reopened what we saw was the values that Guinness stood for represented what people had missed: communion, coming together, quality conversation with friends. We saw that playing out across social media. People were literally posting pictures of their girlfriend with blonde hair and a black turtleneck: ‘Is it just me or does my girlfriend look like a pint of Guinness?’ We leant into that.”

Guinness’s popular post-pandemic “Welcome Back” ad campaign, Wafer said, was “completely inspired by what our drinkers were doing on social media.”

A 1955 Guinness poster by Gilroy, reviving the much-loved toucan he first introduced in 1935.

Wafer and her teams have promoted Guinness as a summer drink, with much success “de-seasonalizing” the brand, as she puts it, opening pop-up bars at festivals and in beer gardens. In Great Britain, they have seen a change in the profile of the typical drinker, moving from a fifth of all Guinness drinkers being women to a quarter. A third of British Guinness drinkers are now between 18 and 34. (The legal age for drinking alcohol in the UK is 18.) How much of this is to do with traditional marketing and how much a result of all those splitting the G videos depends who you ask.

“Look,” she said, “I think our influencers are a big part of our success story. And we work with a lot of influencers. We support and invest in promoting posts. That is important to say. Virality tends to come with paid investment, as well as earned.” Guinness’s ubiquity on social media began as an organic phenomenon, but its continued success has been, at least partly, powered by Diageo money.

Not that Wafer and her teams are relying solely on TikTok to drive further growth. In 2024 Guinness replaced Budweiser as the official beer of the English Premier League, the most watched sports league in the world, at a reported cost of £13mn per season. They have continued to innovate. Guinness Zero, or 0.0, the surprisingly decent no-alcohol version, now accounts for 11% of sales in the UK. Microdraft, a plug-and-play piece of kit that allows a version of draught Guinness to be poured in bars and restaurants, without the need for kegs and cooling equipment, is now delivering Guinness Draught and 0.0 to drinkers in 25 countries. This might be sacrilege to the traditionalists—men like Osh Rogers and Ciaran Kavanagh who have spent many years experimenting with timings, temperature, pressure, nitrogen mix, like mad pub scientists—but, purely for geographical reasons, not everyone can pop into The Gravediggers or The Devonshire for a pint after work.

For home drinkers, Nitrosurge is a clever gadget that attaches to specially adapted cans of Guinness, allowing an approximation of a two-part pub pour in your kitchen. More on this in a moment, when I’ve unboxed it.

All these have contributed to Guinness’s success. So has the fact that the fizz has gone out of the craft beer revolution. Gráinne Wafer thinks an “over-extremism” crept in. “You started to get extremely bitter hoppy crafts.”

Osh Rogers: “I think people drinking IPAs, when they got to the third one they were like, ‘My mouth tastes like the inside of a perfume bottle.’”

The U.S. still has a way to go to catch up with Britain and Ireland. In the UK Guinness makes up 8% of total beer sales, in the U.S. it’s 1%.

As president of Diageo Beer US, Laura Merritt is Guinness’s U.S. boss, based in New York City. She joins my Teams call with Gráinne Wafer. “We’re very much in the throes of a boom but we started from a much smaller base, a more niche place,” she says. “We want our traditional customer to feel welcome but we also want to extend the umbrella to younger people, to women. And to remind them that it doesn’t have to be [consumed] in a pub. It goes great with Chinese food, with Mexican food.”

(This is something that Guinness aficionados I’ve spoken to would not countenance. “Guinness doesn’t like spice, it doesn’t like fried food,” says Ciaran Kavanagh. He also wouldn’t drink it outdoors, especially in summer, believing that direct sunlight, and humidity, react unfavorably with the beer.)

Recent marketing initiatives in the U.S. include a partnership with the boutique ice cream brand Van Leeuwen, exclusively available at Whole Foods Market. It’s called Lovely Day for a Guinness and describes itself as “roasted Guinness stout blended into ultra-creamy ice cream with rich chocolate chunks.” The name comes from one of Guinness’s best-known posters, a 1935 John Gilroy illustration from his zoo-animal series, in which a hapless keeper keeps losing his animals to the lure of a pint.

“We have so much runway for continued discovery,” Merritt says. “We have 375 million Americans and it’s really just the beginning of the journey.”

On this, she and Osh Rogers of The Devonshire are in transatlantic accord. “In the U.S.?” he says, suddenly coming over all Ronald Reagan, “They ain’t seen nothing yet!”

“This will probably be the year that Guinness loses its cool,” wrote Lauren O’Neill, in The Guardian, in 2025. Her diagnosis: “Overexposure, the death knell for cool.”

At any party, there’s typically at least one pooper keen to prick the balloon. So far, O’Neill’s doomy prognostication has not come to pass. And yet, I suggest to Osh Rogers, what goes up must come down. Any product that becomes as hot as Guinness has been in the past few years can only cool in time.

“When a product gets too hot, that’s hype,” he says. “What hype is, is an overplaying of the story against a product which is not quite as good as it says it is. In my experience [as a publican] we had hype with Aperol Spritz, with Bacardi Breezer. We had hype with Sol [Mexican lager], with the lime in it, and with Magners Cider.” Those products, Rogers argues, are unlike Guinness. They appealed only briefly to a specific demographic—young drinkers, easily swayed by passing trends—and did not penetrate the wider public consciousness.

“In the case of Guinness,” Rogers says, “I think the product is as good as it says it is. And maybe it will plateau and fall off in a period of time but right now, we’re going into our fourth Christmas at The Devonshire and Guinness is still growing. I thought we were at capacity two years ago and we’re 35% higher than we were then in volume.

“In 2014 [the year of the negative Mintel report] almost everybody that was drinking Guinness was blokes over 50. Whereas now, I have never seen such an eclectic mix of people drinking the same thing. You look down there [onto the floor of The Devonshire]: it’s tourists, it’s locals, it’s people who work round here, and women and men of all ages and demographics and origins.” As for the future, Rogers describes himself as “very bullish.”

“We still see a lot of headroom for growth,” says Diageo’s Gráinne Wafer. “Even in the UK where we’re the number one beer brand, we see enormous opportunity to drive more scale, especially outside London. In terms of innovation Guinness 0.0 isn’t yet on tap [in more than a handful of bars] and yet it’s already the number one non alc[ohol] beer in the UK.”

She continues: “In North America, beyond the strongholds of New York and Boston and Chicago, there’s enormous headroom for growth. And that’s before I even talk about beer markets in continental Europe like France and Germany, where we’re still sub-scale.”

Diageo has recently confirmed plans for a €400mn investment in a new brewery at Littleconnell in County Kildare, dedicated to the production of Guinness and Guinness 0.0. This does not indicate a lack of confidence in continued demand for the black stuff.

It would be inaccurate to say it’s undisputed, because it’s very much disputed, like everything else concerning Guinness. But ask a selection of connoisseurs, survey the online forums, watch the YouTube videos, and you’ll learn that the best pint of Guinness in Dublin—which is, by most definitions, the best pint of Guinness in Ireland, and therefore the best pint of Guinness in the world—is to be found at a pub called John Kavanagh, in the north western suburb of Glasnevin. It is also known as The Gravediggers.

According to the Guinness Guru, The Gravediggers represents “the holy grail for Guinness aficionados, top of the pops, the crème de la crème.” He awarded it a score of 9.3, confirming its position as “the best pub on the planet.”

ShitLondonGuinness calls The Gravediggers “the best pint of Guinness in the world, bar none.”

It’s a part of who we are. It’s a legacy that goes all the way back, not just to my dad but through the generations. It’s a duty of care with the product. We get a delivery twice a week. We have a storeroom which is 12 to 16 degrees max. The kegs stay a day or two there. From there they go into a cold room at seven degrees. On the fifth or sixth day the keg will be tapped. Then into an ice cooler box, in very cold water. From there to the tap. There’s a pint and a half draw-distance in the line.

– Ciaran Kavanagh

Anthony Bourdain, who visited in 2012 for the Dublin episode of his TV show, The Layover, signed the guestbook “heaven looks just like this.” Bourdain, the late chef turned writer and broadcaster, visited a number of establishments in Dublin on that trip, but the one he fell in love with was The Gravediggers.

“The difference between the stuff here and the indifferently poured swill you get where you come from,” Bourdain said, “is like night and day.”

Diageo, as you might expect, pushed back hard against this idea. “It is a myth,” said Laura Merritt, that Guinness outside Ireland is, by definition, inferior. “You can get an amazing pint of Guinness anywhere on Planet Earth as long as it is the right temperature and the lines are clean and there’s the same amount of care that happens in Ireland. All our Guinness Draught in the U.S. is brewed right there at St James’s Gate in Dublin so it’s the exact same liquid. We have an amazing brand ambassador who travels all over the U.S. to ensure that people are pouring Guinness the exact right way at the exact right temperature with the exact right gas mix. And when that’s done, I would put a pint at The Dead Rabbit [in lower Manhattan] against any pint on the planet.”

Fighting talk from New York! And London?

Osh Rogers of The Devonshire has done as much as anyone to disprove the idea of the inherent superiority of Guinness in Ireland, and even he prefers the beer there.

“We’ve done the best iteration of Guinness that could be done in the UK,” he tells me of The Devonshire. “And people tell us it’s as good as you get in Ireland. I’m not 100% on that. The actual beer in the glass, with the consistency and the head and the dome and the temperature and the flavor? Indistinguishable. [But] I prefer a pint at home [in Dublin], in a pub where it’s been brilliant for 100 years. I prefer that timelessness.”

On a Thursday in June, under leaden skies, I made my pilgrimage, crossing the Liffey from my city center hotel and striding north through a soupy afternoon. There’s little prepossessing about the residential streets of Glasnevin but upon entering Prospect Square a hush seemed to descend, and I could hear only birdsong as I inspected the splendid gates of the Glasnevin Cemetery.

John Kavanagh—The Gravediggers—is built into the wall of the cemetery and since 1833 it has done a brisk trade with the men who give it its nickname and with the mourners who attend funerals and wakes, or simply pass by on their way to visit the grave of a lost loved-one.

The pub has been in the Kavanagh family for nine generations. Ciaran Kavanagh, a widely traveled chef, runs it now with his siblings, following the death of their father Eugene, 10 years ago. The pub is cozy, traditional, wood paneled, immediately convivial, and it is a proper wet-bar: you’re welcome to eat, but to do so you must go next door to the restaurant, where Ciaran himself is in charge of the menu. The pub is for drinking.

Unlike the cliched image of the Irish pub, The Gravediggers does not allow music, singing, or dancing. There’s no TV. There’s no WiFi. The idea, not a new one but much more radical in our smartphone age, is to disconnect completely from the outside world, for an hour or an evening, and indulge in some IRL social networking.

In the snug bar—to which, well into the 1970s, women drinkers were restricted, in pubs across Ireland—Ciaran and I settled in with our first pint of the evening. I asked him why the Guinness at The Gravediggers tastes so good.

“It’s a part of who we are,” he said. “It’s a legacy that goes all the way back, not just to my dad but through the generations. It’s a duty of care with the product. We get a delivery twice a week. We have a storeroom which is 12 to 16 degrees max. The kegs stay a day or two there. From there they go into a cold room at seven degrees. On the fifth or sixth day the keg will be tapped. Then into an ice cooler box, in very cold water. From there to the tap. There’s a pint and a half draw-distance in the line.” Guinness comes every three to four weeks to clean the lines.

“A lot of issues with Guinness when you pour it—the head dying quickly, no lacing [the shtick]—are down to glass hygiene. Wash it with soap. Then the machine wash, 85 to 90 Celsius. Then sterilize, then drip dry. No chemicals.”

Kavanagh prefers the double-pour. “We were always brought up to pour just over the harp. It gives you a chance to look at the beer, see how it’s behaving.”

I’m not one of those with an enormous capacity for beer. Three pints is typically about my limit, and that only on rare occasions, before I’ll switch to something shorter. But so smoothly did these pints go down that I found myself with a fourth and then a fifth, and now I was deep in conversation with a man from Wisconsin, a teacher of creative writing, who was wearing a fishing hat.

As we stood at the bar in companionable commerce, a gentleman from the cemetery, beaming with bonhomie, commandeered my notepad and Biro to write down some of his rules of conduct at The Gravediggers.

His rules ranged from blandly transferable matters of etiquette (“Always say thank you”) to comic-eccentric self-care tips (“Always wash under your neck”) to firmly underlined culinary preferences (“No chips”). There was romantic/financial advice of the kind for which dubious influencers charge younger men than me exorbitant fees: “Stay single and your pockets will jingle.” There were proverbial inducements to order another pint: “A bird never flew on one wing.” There were rules for drinking and for living: “It’s not a race to the finish.” That last one achieved a strange profundity, especially after five pints, especially when standing next door to one of Europe’s largest burial grounds.

And then, perhaps inevitably, there was the one that is most germane to this article: “No splitting the G.”

A Christmas poster by Gilroy. In 1955, Guinness also made its television debut, with an advert broadcast on the opening night of British commercial television.

In order to maintain the trajectory of Guinness, Diageo needs to innovate still more. The idea that Guinness can be enjoyed at home is not a new one. Much of the company’s innovation in recent decades has been driven by this idea. In 1988 Guinness pioneered the “widget”—a groundbreaking technology in which a small plastic ball holds nitrogen under pressure. When the internal pressure is released, by pulling on the ring to open the can, the gas recreates the surge of draught Guinness, causing the creamy head. No one I spoke to for this story who doesn’t work for Diageo felt that canned Guinness could adequately recreate the pub experience. It was fine, rather than sublime.

In 2025 Guinness launched a new product, the Guinness Draught Nitrosurge, under the slogan Connect Activate Pour. This plug-in appliance arrives in a package about the size of a wristwatch box. Separately, I bought a Tulip glass on Amazon which my children are forbidden to fill with alien liquids such as Dr Pepper. You pull the tab, fix the device to the top of the can, press the button, tilt and pour.

In order to recreate the atmosphere at The Gravediggers I would have had to invite 40 or so good-natured people into my kitchen, to drink Nitrosurge pints alongside me. But where would I find the maverick Irish cemetery worker and the soft-spoken American man of letters? My son is a hugely entertaining conversationalist but he’s 13 and while I like to think of myself as a fairly laid-back parent, I think we should wait at least another year or so before we start drinking seriously together.

Still, I enjoyed my first pint of Nitrosurge Guinness. And I suppose that’s the genius of megabrands. They are adaptable to circumstance. And while its popularity may surge and settle, like the beer itself, Guinness has been around for almost 270 years. Hot, cool, or cold, it’s likely to be around for many more.

Sláinte to that.
